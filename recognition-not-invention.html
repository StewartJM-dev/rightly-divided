Rightly Divided — Article Seven

Recognition, Not Invention

A group of men in a room did not decide what the Bible is. They documented what the church had already known for two centuries. Here is exactly how that happened.

By John Stewart

Of all the suspicions people carry about the Bible, this one is the most widespread and the most understandable. Somewhere along the way — through popular novels, documentary films, and casual conversation — millions of people absorbed the idea that a group of powerful men sat in a room, reviewed the available religious texts, voted on which ones they liked, and threw the rest away. The canon of Scripture, on this telling, is the product of human politics rather than divine guidance.

It is worth taking that suspicion seriously rather than dismissing it. Because the honest answer to it is not defensive. It is fascinating. And it is far more compelling than the conspiracy theory it replaces.

The Counterfeit Question

How do you train someone to spot a counterfeit?

The United States Secret Service trains agents to detect counterfeit currency not by showing them every possible variation of a fake bill. They train them by immersing them in the genuine article. Agents handle real money until they know its weight, its texture, its color shifts, its microprinting, the way the ink sits on the paper. They become so familiar with the authentic that the moment a counterfeit enters their hands something is wrong before they can articulate why.

The early church did not need a council to tell them which books were Scripture. They had been handling the genuine article for two centuries. The four Gospels had been read aloud in their gatherings since the first century. Paul's letters had been copied and circulated among churches during Paul's own lifetime. The living chain of testimony ran from the apostles through their disciples through communities that had built their entire existence around specific texts.

By the time the councils met to formally recognize the canon, the church already knew what was real. The councils did not create that knowledge. They documented it. The difference between those two things is the difference between a counterfeiter choosing what currency looks like and a bank examiner certifying what it already is.

The Myth That Must Be Addressed First

The Most Common Misconception

The Council of Nicaea Did Not Choose the Bible

The most persistent myth in popular religious history is that the Emperor Constantine and the Council of Nicaea in 325 CE assembled the books of the Bible, voted on which ones to include, and suppressed the rest. This claim has been repeated so widely — most famously in Dan Brown's novel The Da Vinci Code — that millions of people accept it as historical fact.

It is not. The Council of Nicaea dealt with one primary theological question — the nature of Christ in response to the Arian controversy. The council produced the Nicene Creed. It organized church structure and addressed the date of Easter. It did not produce a canon list. It did not vote on which books belonged in the Bible. The question of the biblical canon was simply not on the agenda at Nicaea.

By 325 CE the four Gospels, Paul's thirteen letters, Acts, First Peter, and First John had been in use as authoritative Scripture across the church for nearly three hundred years. They did not need a council to establish them. The church had been reading, copying, preaching, and dying for these specific texts since the first century. Constantine did not give the church its Bible. He found a church that had already been living with its Bible for generations.

The Three Criteria — How the Church Recognized the Genuine Article

The councils that did formally address the canon were not operating without a standard. They were applying criteria that had been in use since the earliest generations of the church — the natural questions any honest community asks when evaluating a claim to authority. These criteria were not invented by committees. They were the formal expression of what the church had been doing instinctively since the apostles first put pen to parchment.

The Three Tests of Canonicity
1

Apostolicity — Who Wrote It?

Was it written by an apostle or someone in direct apostolic authority? This was the primary question. The apostles were the eyewitnesses — the men who had been with Jesus, had seen the risen Christ, and had been commissioned to establish the church. A document with no traceable connection to apostolic authority had no claim to canonical status. This is the chain of custody argument applied to theology. The same question we asked about the Book of Enoch — where does it come from and can you demonstrate that — was the first question the church asked about every candidate for the canon.

2

Orthodoxy — Does It Agree With the Established Testimony?

Does the book agree with what is already recognized as authoritative Scripture and with the rule of faith — the regula fidei — that the apostles had passed on? This is the consistency test. A document that contradicted the established testimony on essential matters of God, Christ, salvation, and the nature of humanity disqualified itself. The Gnostic gospels failed this test directly — their theology contradicted the apostolic testimony at foundational points. A bank examiner who finds a bill whose color, weight, and microprinting don't match the genuine article does not need to catalogue every way it is wrong. One clear mark of the genuine is sufficient to identify the counterfeit.

3

Catholicity — Has the Church Received It?

Was it universally received and used by the churches across different regions, cultures, and traditions? This is the living transmission argument. A document that had been read publicly in gatherings, copied and circulated, built into the liturgy and teaching of churches across the ancient world carried the weight of communal recognition that no council could confer by vote. The council could only document what the church had already received. This criterion is why the transmission argument matters so much — a text preserved by a living community that read it publicly, built their faith on it, and died rather than surrender it carries an entirely different weight from a text buried in a jar in the desert.

The Undisputed Core — Where Every Examiner Agreed

The councils started where the church already was. Not with a blank slate. With a living community that had been handling specific texts for two centuries. Certain books were received universally from the earliest period without serious question. These were the genuine article against which everything else was measured.

The Books That Were Genuinely Debated — And Why

Here is where honesty matters most. The process was not clean and simple. There were real debates about specific books. Understanding why those debates happened — and what ultimately resolved them — reveals a community doing careful, rigorous intellectual work rather than rubber-stamping a predetermined list.

The debates are not evidence of a flawed or politically motivated process. They are evidence of intellectual seriousness. A community that asked hard questions about authorship, consistency, and reception before accepting a book as authoritative Scripture is a community doing exactly what the counterfeit examiner does — applying known criteria to identify what is genuine. It is the absence of debate that would be suspicious.

Books That Were Used But Not Included

Some books were widely read and valued in the early church but ultimately not received as canonical. Their exclusion was not suppression. It was honest evaluation against known criteria.

The Shepherd of Hermas was popular enough that Origen considered it might be canonical. It was eventually excluded because it was clearly composed in the second century — after the apostolic period — and did not pass the apostolicity test. It was not hidden. It continued to be read for edification. It simply was not Scripture.

The Epistle of Barnabas and First Clement — both valuable early Christian documents — were excluded for the same reason. They were post-apostolic compositions. They passed the orthodoxy and catholicity tests but failed apostolicity. The examiner who can recognize the genuine article identified them as edifying but not authoritative.

The Didache — the Teaching of the Twelve Apostles — is another early document valued but not canonical. These exclusions demonstrate something important. The process was not about what the councils liked. It was about what met the criteria. Books that were orthodox and valued were still excluded if they could not demonstrate apostolic origin.

The Councils — Specifically

With that foundation established, here is exactly what the councils did and when.

The Canon Recognition Timeline

Athanasius's 39th Festal Letter

367 CE — Alexandria, Egypt

Not a council but the single most significant document in New Testament canon history. Bishop Athanasius of Alexandria — the man who held the line against Arianism at the cost of five exiles — wrote his annual Easter letter to his churches listing exactly the 27 books of the New Testament we have today. This is the first time in history that precisely our 27-book New Testament appears as a defined list. Athanasius did not invent this list. He was documenting what the church across the eastern Mediterranean had already been receiving. His authority and precision gave the list enduring weight.

Council of Laodicea

~363 CE — Laodicea, Asia Minor

Canon 60 of this regional council produced a list of books approved for public reading in churches. The New Testament list is essentially our 27 books with one significant exception — Revelation is absent. The Eastern church's hesitation about Revelation due to its misuse by fringe groups is reflected here. The Old Testament list follows the Hebrew canon. This council did not create the canon. It documented what was already in use in the churches of Asia Minor.

Council of Rome

382 CE — Rome

Under Pope Damasus I this council produced the Damasine List — a canon list matching our current 66-book Bible including all 27 New Testament books. This is the Western church formally documenting the same recognition the Eastern church was arriving at simultaneously from a different direction. Jerome, commissioned by Damasus to produce the Latin Vulgate, worked from this recognition. He was careful in his translations to distinguish clearly between the canonical Hebrew Old Testament and the deuterocanonical books, noting the latter were for edification but not for establishing doctrine.

Council of Hippo

393 CE — Hippo, North Africa

A regional North African council attended by Augustine that formally affirmed the 27-book New Testament canon. Augustine — the most influential theologian of the Western church — was present and in agreement. This council represents the African church's formal documentation of what it had already been receiving. Africa was an independent witness to the canon — geographically and culturally distinct from Rome and Asia Minor — and its agreement confirms that the recognition was not a Roman imposition but a convergent conclusion arrived at by different communities handling the same genuine article.

Council of Carthage

397 CE — Carthage, North Africa

The most commonly cited council in discussions of New Testament canon formation. It reaffirmed the decisions of Hippo and produced a definitive list of canonical books consistent with what Athanasius had documented thirty years earlier. Augustine again present. The canon was now formally documented in both East and West, in Africa and Rome, by independent communities that had arrived at the same conclusion through the same process — recognizing what they had already been receiving from the apostolic period.

Council of Carthage

419 CE — Carthage, North Africa

A follow-up council reaffirming the 397 decisions. The repetition is itself significant — this was not a politically imposed decision being enforced from the top down. It was a community continuously confirming what it already held, deepening its documentation of a recognition that had existed for centuries.

What About the Apocrypha — Was It Suppressed?

The honest answer is no. And the evidence is Jerome himself.

Jerome — the most important biblical scholar of the fourth century, the man commissioned by Pope Damasus to produce the authoritative Latin translation — personally translated the deuterocanonical books and included them in the Vulgate. But he labeled them explicitly as a separate category from the canonical Hebrew Old Testament. His prefaces to those books are transparent about their status — useful for edification, part of the church's reading tradition, but not belonging to the Hebrew canon that established the doctrinal foundation.

That is not suppression. That is the most intellectually honest handling possible. Make the books available. Read them. Benefit from them. And be honest about what category they belong in. A bank that labels a bill as a collectible reproduction is not suppressing it. It is handling it truthfully.

The deuterocanonical books remained in Catholic Bibles precisely because they were never suppressed. They were honestly labeled and continuously available. The Protestant Reformers followed Jerome's own distinction — removing the deuterocanonicals from canonical status while acknowledging their value for reading. The books were available to anyone who wanted them. They were simply honest about what they were.

Why This Matters

The suspicion that powerful men invented the canon is understandable but historically backwards. The power in the process belonged not to the councils but to the texts themselves — to the living transmission of a community that had been reading, copying, preaching, and dying for specific books for two centuries before any council convened.

The councils were not above the text. They were under it. Their task was not to decide what God's Word was. It was to document, with as much precision as they could, what the church had already received from the apostolic period — applying criteria that the apostles themselves had implicitly established by the nature of what they wrote and commissioned.

The debates about Hebrews, James, Revelation, and Second Peter were not signs of a corrupt or politically motivated process. They were signs of intellectual integrity. A community willing to ask hard questions about authorship and reception, and to hold books to a consistent standard, is a community whose conclusions can be trusted more than one that accepts everything without examination.

The books that passed all three criteria — apostolic origin, consistency with established Scripture, universal reception by the church — are the 66 books. The books that failed one or more were not hidden or destroyed. They were honestly evaluated and honestly labeled. Some, like Sirach and First Maccabees, continue to be read and valued for exactly what they honestly claimed to be — good books written by honest men in the silence between the testaments.

The canon is not the invention of a council. It is the recognition of a living community trained by two centuries of handling the genuine article to know immediately — before any committee met, before any vote was cast — what was real and what was not.

The counterfeits never had a chance.
